# The Last Good Line — Story Outline

## Premise

Long before the game begins, an unnamed Artist opens a sketchbook and tries to design a tiny fighting game. The Artist deliberately chooses a stickman because a simple hero feels achievable.

That hero is **Line**.

The Artist draws Line's body, experiments with movement poses, puts **Scrapsteel** in his hand, sketches a shield for blocking, and fills the margins with notes about bosses, upgrades, a shopkeeper and future mechanics. One note reads: **FINISH THIS SOMEDAY.**

Someday never comes.

The sketchbook is closed and abandoned. Over the years, all of the crossed-out characters, rejected mechanics and erased drawings remain trapped inside its pages. Their resentment and the Artist's repeated erasing collect at the Back Cover until the discarded material becomes a single destructive force.

When that force — **the Erasing** — reaches the First Page, Line moves for the first time without the Artist drawing him.

The surviving handwritten development notes, called **the Margin**, become Line's guide.

## Chapter I — The First Page

Line wakes and learns the basic combat the Artist originally designed for him.

The Board Shield is not a random pickup; it is the same shield the Artist drew beside Line while experimenting with defensive mechanics.

Line meets **Nib**, a shopkeeper originally drawn as a throwaway joke labeled “SHOP GUY??”. Nib survived because nobody ever bothered to erase him properly.

The chapter ends with **The Scribbled Brute**, an early boss design repeatedly redrawn and finally rejected as “TOO UGLY.” The Brute hates Line because Line's simple design was kept while the Brute was crossed out.

After the fight, the Margin reveals that Line was not the first hero drawn in the book.

## Chapter II — Red Ink

Line enters pages belonging to the **Ink Queen**, an elaborate villain the Artist abandoned for being “too much.” She has spent years finishing herself without the Artist.

This chapter introduces ranged enemies and relics. Relics are revealed to be surviving pieces of cut mechanics: props, timers, strange objects and systems with no original game left to belong to.

The Ink Queen initially sees Line as the Artist's favorite creation. Eventually she realizes Line was abandoned too.

Before disappearing, she warns that the Back Cover is not seeking revenge. It is trying to erase the entire sketchbook so that nothing can ever be rejected again.

## Chapter III — Burnt Pages

These are the pages where the Artist stopped erasing neatly and began tearing or burning bad ideas away.

Line finds the Forge, an unfinished weapon-evolution concept that Nib turns into a functioning system.

Then Line meets the **Margin Knight**.

The Knight is **Hero V1** — the protagonist designed before Line. He had more animation, equipment and complicated mechanics, and the Artist abandoned him because the concept was becoming too difficult to finish. Line was created afterward as a simpler replacement.

The Knight believes Line was chosen because Line was better. Their boss fight is a duel between two versions of the same idea.

After losing, the Knight realizes neither of them was truly rejected in favor of the other. The Artist simply stopped choosing altogether.

He sends Line toward the Back Cover.

## Chapter IV — The Back Cover

There are no development notes here because the Artist never planned anything beyond this point.

The Back Cover speaks through stitched-together monsters made from rejected attacks, unused weapons and crossed-out bodies.

Its argument is simple: the Artist kept one clean line while throwing away an entire world of ideas.

The Margin's final message to Line is:

> You were never the best drawing. You were simply the one still willing to move.

Line fights the Back Cover without the Margin telling him what to do.

After winning, Line refuses to erase the discarded ideas inside it. Instead, he draws a boundary around them, giving the abandoned creations space to exist without consuming the rest of the book.

The Margin asks whether Line is still there.

Line scratches a single line beneath the question.

**Yes.**

In the corner, beside the Artist's old note “maybe finish someday,” the Margin writes:

**ONGOING.**

## Tone

The story should stay compact. It can be funny, strange and self-aware, but the characters should still take their own existence seriously. Cinematics should usually take seconds rather than minutes, and every story scene should either reveal character/world information or introduce something the player is about to use.
